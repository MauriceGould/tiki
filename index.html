Tiki History

More Than a Tropical Drink

Tiki culture in the United States began taking shape in the 1930s, inspired by romanticized ideas of Polynesia and the South Pacific. One of the earliest and most influential figures was Ernest Raymond Gantt, better known as Donn Beach, who opened Don the Beachcomber in Hollywood in 1933. His restaurant combined tropical décor, exotic imagery, and elaborate rum cocktails to create an immersive escape from everyday life. Other restaurants soon followed, helping turn Tiki into a recognizable style of food, drinks, art, and entertainment. Tiki culture grew dramatically during and after World War II. Thousands of American service members spent time throughout the Pacific and returned home with stories, souvenirs, and memories of the region. At the same time, movies, music, restaurants, and advertisements presented Americans with an idealized version of island life. During the 1950s and 1960s, Tiki bars and restaurants became popular across the country, filled with carved figures, bamboo, tropical plants, colorful drinks, and dramatic décor. By the 1970s, Tiki's popularity began to fade as tastes and trends changed. Many famous Tiki restaurants closed or were redesigned, and the style became associated with an earlier era. However, it never disappeared completely. Collectors, bartenders, artists, and enthusiasts continued preserving vintage menus, mugs, recipes, artwork, and stories from Tiki's golden age. Today, Tiki culture has experienced a major revival. Modern Tiki bars celebrate the creativity of classic cocktails and immersive design while also taking a closer look at the history behind the style. Because American Tiki borrowed heavily from many different Pacific cultures—often mixing them together or relying on stereotypes—its history can be both celebrated and critically examined. Understanding that complicated background helps tell a fuller story of how Tiki became such a distinctive part of American pop culture.
